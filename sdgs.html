About the SDGs

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) are 17 global goals adopted by all 193 UN Member States in 2015, designed to address the most pressing challenges facing our world.

These goals are interconnected — solving one often helps solve others. Improving education can help end poverty, and addressing climate change requires global cooperation and innovation.

Member SDG Research Reports

Each Team Member selected a different Global Goal for in-depth research, analyzing social issues related to the goal and proposing solutions.

Li Shuhang

SDG 13 · Climate Action — Climate Action

1. Core Purpose of SDG 13

SDG 13 calls for "urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts." Climate change is a global challenge that affects every country, every community, and every person. The core objectives of SDG 13 include: strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards and natural disasters in all countries; integrating climate change measures into national policies, strategies, and planning; improving education and awareness-raising on climate change mitigation, adaptation, and impact reduction (UN, 2015). This goal is closely aligned with the Paris Agreement, which sets the goal of keeping global warming well below 2°C and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.

2. The Impact of Climate Change on Chinese Coastal Cities

China's coastal areas are facing severe challenges brought by climate change, the most prominent of which are rising sea levels and frequent extreme weather events. According to the China Sea Level Bulletin published by the Ministry of Natural Resources in 2023, China's coastal sea level rose at an average rate of 3.5 mm per year from 1980 to 2022, higher than the global average. Major coastal cities such as Shanghai, Tianjin, and Guangzhou face increasingly severe flood risks (IPCC, 2023).

The causes of this adverse situation include: continuous increase in global greenhouse gas emissions leading to climate warming, glacier melting, and thermal expansion of seawater; rapid urbanization in China's coastal areas increasing the population and asset exposure to climate risks; aging flood control infrastructure in some cities that struggles to cope with increasingly frequent extreme weather events. As an environmental science researcher, Li Shuhang is particularly concerned with how carbon emission trends amplify these risks and how nature-based solutions can build resilience. Affected groups include coastal city residents (especially low-income communities), workers dependent on coastal fisheries and tourism, and coastal ecosystems.

The adverse impacts of climate change on coastal cities are multidimensional. At the economic level, flood disasters cause huge property losses — in 2022 alone, direct economic losses from meteorological disasters in China exceeded 300 billion RMB. At the social level, extreme weather threatens residents' lives and disrupts normal daily life. At the ecological level, rising sea levels exacerbate coastal erosion, wetland degradation, and biodiversity loss (UNEP, 2023).

3. Driving Change: An Action Plan from Climate Vulnerability to Climate Resilience

To address the climate change challenges facing coastal cities and drive the transition from "climate vulnerability" to "climate resilience," the following actions are needed:

1. Build Nature-Based Protective Infrastructure: Promote the "Sponge City" concept by protecting and restoring natural ecosystems such as coastal wetlands, mangroves, and coral reefs to construct green coastal protection belts. Compared with traditional concrete seawalls, nature-based solutions are lower in cost and more sustainable, while also providing co-benefits such as biodiversity conservation and carbon sequestration.

2. Establish a City-Level Climate Risk Early Warning and Emergency Response System: Leverage big data, artificial intelligence, and IoT technologies to build an intelligent climate risk monitoring and early warning platform. Integrate multi-source data including meteorological, hydrological, and geographic information to achieve precise prediction and rapid response to disasters such as floods and typhoons. At the same time, regularly organize community emergency drills to enhance public awareness of disaster prevention and mitigation and self-rescue capabilities.

3. Promote Green Industrial Transformation in Coastal Cities: Develop carbon emission peaking and carbon neutrality roadmaps for coastal cities, accelerating the energy structure transition — vigorously develop offshore wind power, tidal energy, and other marine renewable energy sources. Implement strict emission limits on high-carbon industries while encouraging enterprises to invest in clean technologies and circular economy models through fiscal subsidies and tax incentives.

References: UN (2015). Paris Agreement. | IPCC (2023). Sixth Assessment Report. | UNEP (2023). Emissions Gap Report 2023. | Ministry of Natural Resources of China (2023). China Sea Level Bulletin.

Feng Jingyi

SDG 9 · Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure — Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure

1. Core Purpose of SDG 9

SDG 9 aims to "build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialization, and foster innovation." Infrastructure and industry are the backbone of modern economies — they provide the foundation for productivity, trade, and employment. The core objectives of SDG 9 include: developing quality, reliable, sustainable, and resilient infrastructure to support economic development and human well-being; promoting inclusive and sustainable industrialization, aiming to significantly raise industry's share of employment and GDP; increasing access of small-scale enterprises to financial services and their integration into value chains; and enhancing scientific research and upgrading technological capabilities (UN, 2015). Innovation is the key multiplier in this equation — it determines whether infrastructure becomes smarter, greener, and more accessible over time.

2. The Digital Divide and Smart Infrastructure Gap in Urbanizing Asia

Rapid urbanization across Asia has created a paradox: gleaming smart cities coexist with underserved peri-urban areas that lack basic digital infrastructure. According to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU, 2023), approximately 2.6 billion people globally remain offline, with the majority concentrated in developing regions of Asia and Africa. In Southeast Asia alone, while urban internet penetration exceeds 80%, rural connectivity often falls below 40%. This digital divide directly limits access to e-commerce, digital education, telemedicine, and smart government services.

The causes of this infrastructure gap are structural and technological. First, investment in broadband and smart grids has been concentrated in profitable urban corridors, leaving rural and peri-urban areas underserved — the return-on-investment calculus alone cannot close this gap without public intervention. Second, many existing infrastructure systems were designed in the pre-digital era and lack the sensor networks, data pipelines, and interoperability standards needed for intelligent management. Third, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) — which account for over 60% of employment in most Asian economies — often lack access to affordable technological upgrades, trapping them in low-productivity cycles. As a technology researcher, Feng Jingyi focuses on how intelligent environmental platforms and accessible IoT architectures can bridge this divide, bringing the benefits of smart infrastructure to communities that need them most.

The consequences of the infrastructure-innovation gap are far-reaching. Economically, regions without reliable digital infrastructure struggle to attract investment and diversify beyond primary industries. Environmentally, legacy infrastructure systems — from power grids to water networks — operate with significant inefficiencies, wasting energy and resources. Socially, the digital divide deepens existing inequalities, as those without connectivity are progressively excluded from economic opportunities, civic participation, and access to essential services (World Bank, 2023).

3. Driving Change: From Infrastructure Divide to Inclusive Innovation

To close the digital and infrastructure gap and achieve SDG 9 targets, the following technology-driven action plan can be implemented:

1. Deploy Low-Cost IoT Networks for Smart Infrastructure Monitoring: Develop and deploy open-source, low-power IoT sensor networks that can be retrofitted onto existing infrastructure — water pipes, power lines, bridges, and public buildings — at a fraction of the cost of proprietary systems. These networks provide real-time data on usage, leakage, structural health, and energy consumption, enabling predictive maintenance and resource optimization. Pilot projects in Indonesia and Vietnam have shown that such systems can reduce water leakage by 20–30% and energy waste by 15–25% (UNIDO, 2023).

2. Build Accessible Innovation Hubs for SME Digital Transformation: Establish community-based technology innovation centers that provide SMEs with affordable access to cloud computing, data analytics tools, prototyping equipment, and technical mentorship. These hubs function as shared infrastructure — spreading the fixed costs of advanced technology across many small enterprises and creating collaborative ecosystems where businesses can co-develop solutions. Priority sectors include precision agriculture, renewable energy integration, and circular-economy manufacturing.

3. Launch Public-Private Digital Inclusion Partnerships: Create blended-finance mechanisms where government subsidies, private investment, and multilateral development funds jointly finance last-mile connectivity projects in underserved areas. Couple infrastructure deployment with digital literacy programs to ensure that connectivity translates into actual usage and economic empowerment. These partnerships should include transparent monitoring frameworks with publicly accessible dashboards tracking progress against SDG 9 indicators, ensuring accountability and enabling adaptive management.

References: UN (2015). The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. | ITU (2023). Global Connectivity Report 2023. | World Bank (2023). Digital Development Overview. | UNIDO (2023). Industrial Development Report.

Wang Luyang

SDG 16 · Peace, Justice & Strong Institutions — Peace, Justice & Strong Institutions

1. Core Purpose of SDG 16

SDG 16 aims to "promote peaceful and inclusive societies for sustainable development, provide access to justice for all, and build effective, accountable, and inclusive institutions at all levels." Peace, justice, and strong institutions are both goals in their own right and essential enablers of every other SDG — without the rule of law and accountable governance, progress on poverty, health, education, and environmental protection remains fragile and reversible. The core targets of SDG 16 include: significantly reducing all forms of violence and related death rates; ending abuse, exploitation, trafficking, and all forms of violence against children; promoting the rule of law and ensuring equal access to justice for all; substantially reducing corruption and bribery; and ensuring responsive, inclusive, participatory, and representative decision-making at all levels (UN, 2015).

2. Access to Justice and Institutional Trust in Rapidly Transforming Societies

Rapid economic transformation creates both opportunities and governance challenges. As societies urbanize and digitalize, traditional mechanisms of accountability and dispute resolution are often disrupted before new institutions can take root. According to the World Justice Project (2023), approximately 5.1 billion people — two-thirds of the global population — face at least one unmet justice need annually, ranging from land disputes and labor rights violations to consumer fraud and domestic violence. The justice gap is widest for women, low-income groups, and rural residents who face compounded barriers of cost, distance, language, and legal literacy.

The causes of weak institutional trust are interconnected. First, in fast-growing economies, regulatory frameworks often lag behind economic change — new sectors like the gig economy and digital finance operate in gray zones with limited worker protections and ambiguous accountability. Second, corruption erodes public confidence: Transparency International (2023) estimates that over 20% of citizens in developing regions report having paid a bribe for public services in the previous year. Third, civic space is constrained in many contexts — civil society organizations face registration barriers, funding restrictions, and surveillance, limiting their ability to serve as watchdogs and accountability partners. Wang Luyang researches how inclusive policy design and multi-stakeholder governance mechanisms can address these challenges, strengthening the institutional fabric that sustainable development depends on.

The consequences of weak institutions extend across all dimensions of development. Economically, corruption and unpredictable legal systems deter investment and misallocate resources. Socially, lack of access to justice leaves the most vulnerable without recourse to assert their rights, deepening inequality. Politically, when institutions are perceived as unaccountable, social cohesion frays and conflict risks rise. The OECD (2023) estimates that countries with the weakest rule of law lose the equivalent of 2–3% of GDP annually to the costs of insecurity, inefficiency, and lost investment.

3. Driving Change: From Institutional Weakness to Inclusive Governance

To strengthen access to justice and build accountable institutions aligned with SDG 16, the following action plan can be implemented:

1. Expand Community-Based Legal Empowerment and Alternative Dispute Resolution: Train and deploy community paralegals — local residents equipped with basic legal knowledge — to provide frontline justice services in underserved areas. These paralegals can handle common disputes (land, family, labor, consumer) through mediation and provide referrals for more complex cases. Evidence from pilot programs in South Africa, Sierra Leone, and Indonesia shows that community paralegal models can resolve 60–80% of cases at a fraction of the cost of formal court proceedings, while also building legal literacy within communities (UNDP, 2023).

2. Deploy Digital Transparency Platforms for Participatory Governance: Build open-data platforms that make government budgets, procurement contracts, and service delivery metrics accessible and understandable to ordinary citizens. Couple these platforms with mobile-based feedback mechanisms that allow citizens to report service failures, suggest improvements, and track government responses. When integrated with independent audit mechanisms and civil society oversight, such platforms have been shown to reduce procurement costs by 8–12% and significantly improve service delivery responsiveness (World Bank, 2023).

3. Strengthen Multi-Stakeholder Accountability and Cross-Border Cooperation: Promote the establishment of regional governance networks that facilitate peer learning, benchmarking, and mutual accountability among countries facing similar institutional challenges. Support civil society organizations through legal recognition, transparent funding mechanisms, and protection from harassment. Engage the private sector through mandatory transparency standards — such as beneficial ownership registries and public country-by-country financial reporting — that reduce corruption risks and level the playing field for responsible businesses.

References: UN (2015). The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. | World Justice Project (2023). Rule of Law Index. | Transparency International (2023). Corruption Perceptions Index. | UNDP (2023). Access to Justice and Rule of Law Report. | OECD (2023). Government at a Glance.

Lu Jianning

SDG 4 · Quality Education — Quality Education

1. Core Purpose of SDG 4

SDG 4 aims to "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all." Education is widely recognized as the most powerful tool for breaking the intergenerational cycle of poverty — each additional year of schooling can increase an individual's future earnings by approximately 10%. Despite significant global progress, 244 million children and youth were still out of school in 2023, and 56% of primary-school-age children worldwide could not read a simple text by age 10. The core objectives of SDG 4 include: ensuring all girls and boys complete free, equitable and quality primary and secondary education; eliminating gender disparities in education; substantially increasing the supply of qualified teachers; and ensuring equal access to affordable technical, vocational and tertiary education (UNESCO, 2023).

2. Educational Inequality: A Data-Driven Diagnosis of Resource Distribution Gaps

Despite continuous growth in global education investment, the inequality of educational resource distribution remains stark. A multidimensional data analysis reveals the following structural challenges:

Urban-Rural Divide: Cross-country data analysis shows that rural children are on average 2–3 times more likely to be out of school than their urban peers. In Sub-Saharan Africa, only 25% of rural primary schools have access to electricity, compared to 78% in urban areas, and the pupil-to-qualified-teacher ratio in rural regions can reach 80:1 versus 35:1 in cities (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2022). The digital divide further exacerbates the gap — during the COVID-19 pandemic, 2.2 billion children globally lacked internet access at home, essentially cutting them off from all learning opportunities.

Girls' Education: While global gender parity in primary enrollment has improved, severe disparities persist at secondary and tertiary levels. In least-developed countries, only 64 girls for every 100 boys are enrolled in upper secondary education. Early marriage remains a major barrier, with approximately 12 million girls married before age 18 each year, often resulting in school dropout. Data models project that achieving universal upper secondary education for girls by 2030 could add an estimated 15–30 trillion USD to global GDP over their lifetimes (World Bank, 2023).

Quality Gap: Beyond enrollment numbers, the "learning crisis" is equally alarming. The World Bank's Learning Poverty indicator reveals that in low-income countries, up to 90% of children cannot read and understand a simple text by age 10. Teacher shortages are acute — an additional 44 million teachers are needed globally to achieve universal primary and secondary education by 2030, with Sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia accounting for two-thirds of this gap. Without data-driven resource reallocation, these regions risk a "lost generation" of human capital.

3. Data-Driven Action Plan: From Diagnosis to Intervention

To close the educational resource gap and achieve SDG 4 targets, the following data-driven action plan can be implemented:

1. Build an Educational Resource Equity Index and Early Warning System: Integrate existing data sources — national education management information systems (EMIS), household surveys, and satellite-based school mapping — to construct a multidimensional educational equity index covering access, quality, infrastructure, and learning outcomes. Deploy predictive analytics to identify "education poverty hotspots" before crises emerge, enabling proactive resource deployment rather than reactive remediation. This index can serve as a transparent accountability tool for governments and international donors.

2. Optimize Resource Allocation with Geospatial and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis: Apply geospatial analysis to map the distance from every community to the nearest school with qualified teachers and adequate facilities. Overlay this with demographic data to quantify the per-capita investment gap at the subnational level. Use cost-effectiveness modeling to compare intervention options — for instance, scholarship programs versus school infrastructure investment versus teacher training — and identify the optimal resource mix for each region's specific constraints and needs.

3. Establish a Real-Time Learning Outcome Monitoring System: Move beyond enrollment-based metrics by implementing sample-based learning assessments that provide timely, disaggregated data on actual learning outcomes. Couple this with a public-facing dashboard that tracks progress against SDG 4 indicators at the national and subnational levels, enabling civil society organizations, researchers, and citizens to hold governments accountable for educational quality, not just access. The system should be designed to integrate with existing UNESCO and World Bank data platforms to avoid duplication and ensure global comparability.

References: UNESCO (2023). Global Education Monitoring Report. | World Bank (2023). The State of Global Learning Poverty. | UNESCO Institute for Statistics (2022). Education Indicators Database. | UN (2015). The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.
